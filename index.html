01 / About

Learning complex ideas should feel rigorous, not inaccessible.

My academic work sits at the intersection of intelligent systems and autonomous aerial systems, especially UAVs and VTOL platforms. I am especially interested in how students move from mathematical and algorithmic ideas to systems they can implement, test, explain, and evaluate critically.

What shapes my classroom

  • Connect theory to authentic engineering problems.
  • Make reasoning visible through discussion and explanation.
  • Use iterative experimentation instead of one-shot answers.
  • Emphasize responsible, interpretable, and robust AI systems.

03 / Teaching Philosophy

From “What is the answer?” to “How do we know?”

My teaching philosophy is centered on active, evidence-based learning. In computer science, understanding is strongest when students can explain a concept, implement it, test it under imperfect conditions, and defend the choices they made.

I want students to leave a course with both technical confidence and intellectual caution: confidence to build intelligent systems, and caution to question assumptions, evaluate failure modes, and recognize when an algorithm should not be trusted.

01

Activate prior knowledge

Start from what students already understand, then create clear bridges to new abstractions.

02

Learn by doing

Use coding, experiments, simulations, and structured problem solving to turn concepts into experience.

03

Discuss decisions

Ask students to explain tradeoffs, compare alternatives, and listen critically to other approaches.

04

Reflect on failure

Treat unexpected results as evidence to investigate, not simply mistakes to erase.

Proposed course · 14 weeks · upper-level undergraduate / graduate

Autonomous Aerial Systems: Perception, Learning & Trust

This proposed course teaches modern AI through one evolving system: an autonomous VTOL that must see, decide, act, and justify its behavior. Each phase adds a layer to the same project, so students experience how design choices compound.

  1. Weeks 1–4Perceive

    Sensors, image formation, and detection and tracking in aerial imagery.

    Lab · Detect landing pads in drone footage
  2. Weeks 5–8Decide

    Markov decision processes, value-based and policy-gradient RL, and reward design.

    Lab · Train a hover-and-land policy in simulation
  3. Weeks 9–11Act safely

    Control loops, VTOL transition, safety constraints, and the sim-to-real gap.

    Lab · Stress-test a policy under wind and sensor noise
  4. Weeks 12–14Trust

    Explainability, adversarial attacks on perception, and robustness evaluation.

    Lab · Red-team a peer’s model, then write an explanation memo
Course learning outcomes
  1. Implement perception and decision modules for an autonomous UAV pipeline.
  2. Design experiments that test robustness, not just average performance.
  3. Explain a learned policy’s behavior using appropriate explainable-AI methods.
  4. Critique the safety and ethical implications of deploying autonomous aircraft.
Assessment mix
  • Weekly labs30%
  • Project milestones35%
  • Explanation memo & live demo25%
  • Exit tickets & reflections10%

Weighted toward process: three of the four components reward iteration and explanation, not only final output.

Sample lesson · 75 minutes · week 3 of the course

“Can the drone trust what it sees?”

Students investigate how a vision model’s confidence changes under blur, glare, and altitude, then design a safe fallback behavior for when perception becomes unreliable.

Learning objectives

By the end of the session, students will be able to:

  1. Explain why a detector’s confidence score is not the probability that it is correct.
  2. Predict how image conditions change detection performance, then test that prediction.
  3. Justify a fallback behavior for a UAV when perception confidence drops.
Inclusive design
  • Starter notebook with a low-floor path and an extension task on calibration curves.
  • Pairs swap driver and navigator roles halfway through the lab.
  • Each concept appears as a diagram, as pseudocode, and as a live run.

Formative assessment · continuous improvement

Collect → analyze → adjust → report back

I treat my own teaching like a system under test: gather evidence from students often, change what the evidence says is not working, and tell students what changed and why.

  1. CollectExit tickets every session; an anonymous Start / Stop / Continue survey at mid-semester.
  2. AnalyzeSort responses by misconception and by theme before the next class.
  3. AdjustOpen the next session by re-teaching the top two confusions with a fresh example.
  4. Report backShare survey themes with the class: what will change, what won’t, and why.

↺ The cycle repeats every week

  • Peer observationInvite a colleague to observe a session with one specific focus question.
  • Reflection journalAfter each session, note what worked, what didn’t, and one change for next time.
  • Assessment auditBefore the term begins, check every graded task against the course outcomes.

Class session plan · 50 minutes · reinforcement learning unit

Teaching a drone to land: MDPs and reward design

This session follows a bookend–overlay–interleave structure. Retrieval practice opens the class, guided notes keep students writing through two short lecturettes, a think-pair-share breaks the lecture at its midpoint, and a lecture wrapper closes it by checking what students actually took away.

Learning objectives

By the end of class, students will be able to:

  1. Formulate a UAV landing task as an MDP by naming its states, actions, rewards, and discount factor.
  2. Predict how a sparse versus a shaped reward changes what a learning agent does.
  3. Explain how an agent can exploit a careless reward, and how shaping can prevent it.
Materials
  • Two-part guided-notes handout, also posted as a fillable PDF.
  • Slides with a 60-second simulation clip of a drone gaming its reward.
  • Index cards, or a two-question LMS form, for the lecture wrapper.
  • A countdown timer on the projector for each activity.
  1. 00–05Bookend
    Update your classmate

    Retrieve the previous class before adding anything new, and surface gaps early.

    Students
    In pairs, Partner A has 90 seconds to explain last class’s big idea, tracking a landing pad across video frames. Partner B adds one detail A missed and writes down one open question.
    Instructor
    Circulates and listens for misconceptions, calls on two pairs for their questions, then bridges to today: “The drone can now see the pad. How should it decide what to do next?”

    EvidenceThe questions pairs raise, and recurring gaps noted for the wrapper.

  2. 05–20Overlay
    Guided notes over lecturette 1: landing as an MDP

    Give students a scaffold for the key structure while they listen, so their attention goes to ideas rather than transcription.

    Lecturette
    The agent–environment loop and the MDP tuple (S, A, P, R, γ), applied to landing: altitude, velocity, pad offset, and battery as state; thrust commands as actions; wind making transitions stochastic.
    Students
    Fill in part 1 of the guided notes. The instructor pauses twice for 60 seconds so students can complete blanks and check them with a neighbor.

    EvidenceA quick scan of notes during each pause, and one blank answered aloud by a volunteer.

  3. 20–25Interleave
    Think-pair-share

    Apply the MDP framing to a concrete case, and set up the problem the second lecturette solves.

    Prompt
    “The drone earns +100 for a safe landing and 0 for everything else. Predict what it does during its first 1,000 training episodes, and explain why.”
    Students
    Think alone and write a prediction (1 min), compare with a partner (2 min), then two or three randomly chosen pairs share (2 min).
    Instructor
    Tallies the predictions on the board (crashes, drifts randomly, learns to land) and leaves the tally up for lecturette 2.

    EvidenceWritten predictions in the guided-notes margin, and the class tally.

  4. 25–40Overlay
    Guided notes over lecturette 2: reward design and reward hacking

    Show why the sparse reward from the think-pair-share learns so slowly, and why a careless fix can be worse.

    Lecturette
    Sparse versus shaped rewards; a 60-second clip of a drone, rewarded for being near the pad, that learns to hover just above it forever; the fix: potential-based shaping, which rewards progress without changing the optimal policy, plus a small time penalty.
    Students
    Fill in part 2 of the guided notes, then return to their think-pair-share prediction and mark whether it held up.

    EvidenceCorrected predictions, and a show-of-hands check after the clip: “Why doesn’t it land?”

  5. 40–45Bookend
    Lecture wrapper

    Check whether what students took away matches what mattered, and build the habit of monitoring their own understanding.

    Students
    On an index card, write the three most important ideas from today and one question that is still open (3 min). Then compare with the instructor’s list: circle the matches and star anything missed.
    Instructor
    Reveals a top three (2 min): a task becomes RL-ready once S, A, R, and γ are named; sparse rewards make learning slow, and shaping helps; an agent optimizes the reward you wrote, not the one you meant.

    EvidenceThe collected cards show which ideas landed. Mismatches become the topic of the next class’s “Update your classmate”.

  6. 45–50Flex
    Flex time

    The five activities total 45 minutes. The last five absorb transitions and overruns, so the closing wrapper is never cut.

    If time is short
    Drop the second notes pause in lecturette 1, never the think-pair-share or the wrapper.
    If time is left
    Remind teams of the next project milestone and take individual questions.
